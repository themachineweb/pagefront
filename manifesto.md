# Authoring, or being inferred

Imagine a web where you don't decide how your business looks. Your site exists, but most of your customers never see it. They see a platform's version: a format you didn't choose, ranked against competitors you didn't agree to, described in words you didn't write.

It's not your imagination. You're being turned into a supplier.

If you put anything on the web, a product, an article, a profile, a service, you are a publisher. The word is shifting, but the act is unchanged: you make something, put it at a stable address, and let people find it on terms you set.

For thirty years that was enough. You published, people came, and your page was where the encounter happened. You chose what they saw.

That is ending. The reader who used to visit your page now asks an AI surface instead. The agent reads on their behalf, summarizes, compares and synthesizes, and the reader gets an answer without ever reaching you. You do not learn that the question was asked. What survives of you is whatever the intermediary chose to surface.

The loss takes two forms. The first is traffic. SEO was a deal: you let the crawlers in, and they sent readers back. That deal is over. The crawler still takes, but the reader stays on the platform. The second is harder to name. Ask an agent about a product and the story collapses to a spec line. The voice, and the reason anyone chose this brand over the next one, are gone. The brand is rendered as a SKU.

In both cases the page on your own domain is no longer where the encounter happens.

Every prior layer consolidated the same way. On Amazon, brands became interchangeable listings in someone else's marketplace, ranked by someone else's rules. In WeChat, the entire user experience was absorbed into one app, until reaching a customer meant existing on terms the superapp set. Each time, publishers traded direct relationships for access to an audience, and the owner of the channel captured the relationship and the know-how. The Machine Web is the next layer, and the same trade is on offer.

Look back at 2026 from ten years on and the question will be why anyone agreed to this. Nobody did. It happened by default, while everyone was busy.

The point the AI conversation keeps missing is that we are all publishers, and we want to take part in the web that machines now read: on our own terms, on our own domains, in our own voice, with our own canonical statement of what we are. That option still exists. By default, it will not for much longer.

The human web was open by design. Its foundations were published in the open, free to implement and owned by no one. Anyone with a domain could publish, and anyone with a browser could read. The largest companies in the world were built on that.

The Machine Web is being built differently. Publishers feed platforms, and platforms decide what readers see. The end state is easy to see: a different format for every gatekeeper, costs that scale with their number, until only the largest publishers can afford to appear everywhere and the rest go silent. Whoever survives owns the catalog, the protocol and the experience.

This is being decided now, in which formats ship next quarter and which protocols agents adopt before anyone notices that a choice was made. It is not being decided in a standards body, or in public.

The Machine Web needs what the human web had: a way to publish directly to machines, on your own domain, built on standards the web already has, without handing control to a platform. It needs structure, so that we can interoperate, and openness, so that anyone can join.

Some of us are building that. If you publish on the web, as a brand, a retailer, a media owner or a content producer, this concerns you. If you build agents that read the web, you have a stake. If you care whether the Machine Web inherits the openness of the human one, this is when that is decided.

We are not buying permission.\
We are publishing on the Machine Web.

— Stephane Lee
